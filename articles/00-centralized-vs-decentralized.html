Centralized vs Decentralized

Before learning what Solana is, it helps to see the problem it addresses. In this file we will send the same $100 abroad twice, first through banks and then through a blockchain, and compare what happens to the money along the way.

  • levelbeginner
  • prerequisitesnone
  • reading time~7 min
  • facts verified18 Nov 2026

01Two ways to organize a system

A centralized system is run by one operator, or by a small group, that keeps the records and decides who can use the system. A bank, a card network and SWIFT all work this way.

A decentralized system is run by many independent operators who use the same software and keep the same records, so no single one of them can decide alone. The validators that run Solana are an example.

Neither design is better in every case. Each one gives up something to gain something else, and the two examples below show what that means in practice.

02Example 1: sending $100 abroad through banks

You live in country A and have an account at Bank A. Your friend lives in country B and has an account at Bank B, and you want to send them $100.

Banks in different countries rarely hold accounts with each other, so the payment travels through one or more correspondent banks that do. In the figure, Bank C plays that role. The banks coordinate through SWIFT, a network that carries payment instructions between banks. SWIFT itself does not move any money: each bank receives the instruction and updates its own records.

Following the $100

The figure uses typical fees from US banks. Here is what happens to the money at each step:

Most of these fees are flat, which means they cost the same whether you send $100 or $20,000. For large transfers they are a small fraction of the amount, but for small ones, like this example, they can take more than half.

How long it takes

The instructions themselves travel quickly: about 90% of cross-border payments on SWIFT reach the receiving bank within an hour. The money reaching your friend's account is a different matter, since only 43% of cross-border payments are completed end to end within an hour. Most of the delay happens at the receiving bank, which runs its own compliance checks, processes payments in batches and only works on business days. A transfer sent on a Friday night can easily wait until Monday.

What if your country is cut off from SWIFT?

Now imagine that your country enters a conflict and, as part of international sanctions, its banks are disconnected from SWIFT.

From that moment Bank A can no longer exchange payment instructions with banks abroad, and the correspondent banks may also freeze the accounts they hold for it. Your $100 has no way out of the country through the usual route, and money sent to you from abroad cannot get in. Paying in a local shop still works, because payments inside the country run on national systems that do not depend on SWIFT.

This is possible because SWIFT is a single organization, based in Belgium and subject to its laws. One legal decision, applied at one point, is enough to remove access for every customer of the affected banks.

Who can stop the payment

Bank A, each correspondent bank, SWIFT, Bank B and the regulators of every country involved can delay, charge or block the transfer. That same control has a useful side: when something goes wrong, a bank can stop a fraudulent payment, fix a mistake or give you a refund.

03Example 2: sending $100 abroad on Solana

This time you and your friend each have a wallet, an app that holds your keys and lets you send and receive funds. You hold 100 USDC, a digital token issued by the company Circle in which each unit is worth one US dollar. Because its value stays at one dollar, your friend receives the same amount you send, without exchange-rate surprises.

What happens in the figure

  1. Your wallet prepares a transaction that says, in short, "move 100 USDC from my account to my friend's account", and signs it with your private key. The signature proves that you, and only you, authorized it.
  2. The wallet sends the transaction to the Solana network, which is made up of many independent computers called validators.
  3. The validator whose turn it is to produce the next block includes your transaction. The other validators check that the signature is valid and that you actually have 100 USDC, and then vote on the block.
  4. Once enough validators agree, the transfer is final. Every validator updates its own copy of the records: your account goes down by 100 USDC and your friend's goes up by 100 USDC.

Nobody between you and your friend takes a cut, so the 100 USDC arrive complete.

What it costs

The network charges 5,000 lamports per signature, which is 0.000005 SOL. To put that in perspective, even if 1 SOL cost $1,000, the fee would be half a cent. When the network is busy, wallets can add a small optional priority fee to move the transaction ahead in line.

Unlike bank fees, this cost does not depend on the amount or on the destination country. There are two small details to keep in mind, though: you need a little SOL in your wallet to pay the fee, even when you send USDC, and the first time your friend receives USDC, a small one-time deposit is needed to create the account that will hold it.

How long it takes

Solana produces a new block every 200 ms and aims to make each block final about 150 ms later, so the transfer is usually complete in less than a second. The network runs 24 hours a day, weekends and holidays included.

What if your country is cut off from SWIFT?

The Solana network does not keep a list of member countries or banks, and there is no central operator with the power to disconnect a participant. Validators are run by independent operators in many countries, and if some of them go offline, even all the ones in a single country, the rest keep producing blocks as long as enough of the network stays online.

What does not change

Moving money on the network is only part of the story, and a few limits remain:

  • Getting money in and out. To turn your local currency into USDC, and back again, you usually go through an exchange. Exchanges are regulated companies, so they can block individual users or whole countries.
  • The token issuer. Circle can freeze the USDC held at specific addresses. The network is decentralized, but that particular token is not.
  • The law. Sanctions and financial regulations apply to people and companies regardless of the technology they use.
  • Nobody to call. If you send funds to the wrong address or lose your private key, there is no bank that can reverse the transfer or recover the money.
  • Price. USDC is designed to stay at one dollar, but if you send SOL instead, its value in dollars can change considerably from one day to the next.

05Summary

In a centralized system, one operator keeps the records and can approve, charge, delay or block each payment. In a decentralized system, many independent operators keep the same records, and none of them can exclude a participant on their own.

Decentralization removes intermediaries and single points of control, which makes transfers cheaper, faster and harder to block. It also removes the protections an intermediary offers, so the responsibility for keys and mistakes moves to you.

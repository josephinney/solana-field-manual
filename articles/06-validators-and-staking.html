Validators and staking

Validators run the network, and anyone who holds SOL decides which of them carry more weight. This file explains what validators do, how they earn, how staking works and how to choose a validator in a way that keeps the network decentralized.

  • levelbeginner
  • prerequisitesnone
  • reading time~8 min

01What a validator does

A validator is a server running the Solana software, operated by a company, a community project or an individual. It has two jobs. Most of the time it checks the blocks produced by others and votes on them. And when its turn comes in the leader schedule, it becomes the leader for four consecutive slots and produces the blocks itself.

Not every vote counts the same. A validator's weight in voting, and how often it becomes leader, depend on its stake: the amount of SOL delegated to it. When Alpenglow went live in October 2026 the network had 654 validators, and most of them hold only a small share of the total stake.

02How validators earn and spend

Time on Solana is also measured in epochs. An epoch lasts 432,000 slots, which at 200 ms per slot is about one day, and it is the unit for everything related to staking: rewards are paid, stake is activated and the leader schedule is set once per epoch.

A validator has three sources of income:

  • Inflation rewards. At the start of every epoch the network creates new SOL and distributes it among validators and their stakers, in proportion to their stake and to how well they voted.
  • Block fees. When it is the leader, a validator keeps half of the base fee and all of the priority fee of every transaction it includes.
  • Tips. Some apps add separate payments to their transactions to get them included sooner.

On the cost side are the servers, which need fast hardware and a lot of bandwidth, and since Alpenglow the Validator Admission Ticket: a flat 0.8 SOL per epoch that each validator pays, and that is burned, to take part in the next epoch. It replaced the vote fees validators used to pay, about 1 SOL per epoch. A validator that cannot pay it is left out of the next epoch and earns nothing during it.

From the inflation rewards of its stakers, each validator keeps a percentage called the commission, usually between 0% and 10%. The rest goes to the people who delegated to it.

03What happens when you stake

Staking means delegating your SOL to a validator. You do not send it to them: you create a stake account, move SOL into it and point it at the validator's vote account.

The stake account has two authorities, normally both your wallet: the stake authority, which can delegate or deactivate the stake, and the withdraw authority, which can take the SOL out. The validator holds neither, so it cannot touch your funds. All it gets is voting weight while your stake is delegated, and its commission on the rewards.

04How much staking earns

Solana's inflation started at 8% per year in 2021 and falls by 15% every year until it reaches a floor of 1.5%. In August 2026 it was around 3.9%, and because only staked SOL receives the new coins, and about two thirds of all SOL is staked, the reward for stakers was higher: about 5.35% per year before commission.

With those numbers, 100 SOL delegated to a validator with a 5% commission would grow by roughly 5 SOL in a year. Rewards are added to the stake account every epoch, so they also earn rewards from then on.

Part of that return is not a gain. New SOL is created for stakers every epoch, so someone who does not stake owns a slightly smaller share of all SOL every day, and staking mostly protects you from that dilution. And the rate is falling: on 28 August 2026 validators voted to double the yearly reduction from 15% to 30%, which will bring inflation to its floor around 2029. As of this writing, the change has been approved but not yet activated.

05Activation, deactivation and withdrawal

Stake does not start or stop working immediately. Every change waits for the next boundary between epochs:

After you delegate, the stake is activating until the next epoch begins, and only then starts earning. When you want your SOL back, you first deactivate it, which also completes at the next epoch boundary, and then you can withdraw it to your wallet. The network also limits how much stake can activate or deactivate in a single epoch, so after very large movements, such as a big holder leaving, the wait can last a few epochs longer.

[i] "insufficient funds" when withdrawing

If you try to withdraw while the stake is still deactivating, the wallet or the CLI returns an error that says "insufficient funds". Your SOL is still there, but it stays staked until the epoch ends, so none of it can be withdrawn yet. Wait for the next epoch and try again.

06Risks

Solana does not currently slash stake, that is, it does not destroy part of a validator's stake as a punishment for misbehaving. The idea is being researched, and if it is ever activated, the choice of validator would become a risk to your funds and not only to your rewards. Today the risks are smaller but real:

  • Missed rewards. If your validator is offline, votes badly or does not pay its admission ticket, you earn little or nothing for that epoch.
  • Commission changes. A validator can raise its commission. Check it from time to time.
  • Time to exit. Your SOL is unavailable while it deactivates, which matters if you need it quickly.
  • Price. Rewards are paid in SOL, so their value in dollars moves with the price of SOL.

There is also liquid staking: you deposit SOL in a stake pool, a program that delegates it across many validators, and you receive a token that represents your share, such as JitoSOL or mSOL. You can sell or use that token without waiting for an epoch, but you add the risk of the pool's program and depend on how it chooses validators.

07Choosing a validator

Your choice of validator also shapes the network itself, because stake decides who controls consensus. The usual measure is the Nakamoto coefficient: the smallest number of validators that together hold a third of the stake, enough to stop the network from finalizing blocks if they stopped together. In October 2026 Solana's was 18.

Every delegation to one of the largest validators makes that group a little more powerful, while every delegation to a smaller, well-run validator spreads the weight. When you choose, look at:

  1. Performance: how reliably it votes and produces blocks. Validator dashboards show it as vote credits or uptime.
  2. Commission: a reasonable, stable percentage. Running a validator costs money, and a validator that charges 0% to attract stake may not be sustainable.
  3. Size: preferably outside the group of largest validators that together hold a third of the stake, often called the superminority.
  4. Diversity: a client, a country and a data center that are less common, so that a single bug or outage affects less of the network.

08Summary

Validators produce blocks and vote, with a weight that depends on the stake delegated to them. Staking lets you lend that weight to a validator without giving up your SOL, in exchange for a share of the new SOL created every epoch. Choosing a good validator outside the largest group earns you rewards and keeps the network decentralized.

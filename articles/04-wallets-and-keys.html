Wallets and keys

A wallet does not hold your money; it holds the keys that control it. This file explains where those keys come from, who can hold them, what you authorize every time you sign, and the tricks people use to make you sign the wrong thing.

  • prerequisitesnone
  • reading time~8 min
  • tested withsolana-keygen 4.2.2
  • facts verified18 Nov 2026

01What a wallet actually does

Your SOL and tokens are records on the network, kept by thousands of validators. A wallet app does three things with them: it stores your private key, it reads the network to show you your balances, and it uses the key to sign transactions when you want to move something.

That has two consequences that surprise most people. If your phone falls into a lake, your funds are safe: you install the wallet on a new phone, restore your key, and everything appears again, because nothing but the key was ever stored on the phone. And if someone else gets your key, they have exactly the same power you have, from any device in the world, without needing your phone or your password.

02The seed phrase

When you create a wallet, the app shows you 12 or 24 common English words, called the seed phrase or recovery phrase. It is the master secret: from those words the wallet calculates your private keys, and from each private key it calculates a public address.

Two details make the seed phrase so important. First, the calculation only works in one direction: knowing an address tells you nothing about its private key, and knowing a private key tells you nothing about the seed phrase. That is why you can share your address freely. Second, a single phrase can generate many accounts, so when your wallet app lets you add "Account 2" or "Account 3", they all come from the same words, and whoever has the words controls all of them.

How to keep it

Write it on paper, or engrave it on metal if you hold a larger amount, and store it somewhere safe and offline. Some people keep two copies in different places in case of fire or flood. Avoid anything that can be copied without you noticing: photos, screenshots, notes apps, cloud storage and email.

[!] the one rule with no exceptions

Nobody legitimate will ever ask for your seed phrase: not a wallet's support team, not an exchange, not an airdrop, not a validator. The only place you ever type it is the restore screen of your own wallet app. Anyone else who asks is trying to steal from you.

With an exchange, your balance is a promise from the company, much like a bank account. With a wallet app or a hardware wallet you hold the key yourself, which is called self-custody: nobody can freeze or take your funds, but nobody can help you either if you lose the seed phrase. A hardware wallet adds one more layer: the key never leaves the device, and every transaction has to be confirmed on its own screen, so even a computer full of malware cannot sign in your name.

04What you approve when you sign

Apps ask for two different things, and it helps to tell them apart. Connecting your wallet only shares your public address, so the app can read your balances; it cannot move anything. Approving or confirming means signing a transaction, and a transaction can contain several instructions, not only the one the app describes.

A transaction you sign can move your SOL and tokens, but it can also give another address permission to move a token later (a delegate), or even change the owner of your account, so that a program controlled by someone else decides what happens to it. A wallet that has been reassigned this way still shows its balance, but you can no longer move the funds. Security researchers at SlowMist documented this technique in a phishing campaign that stole about $3 million.

Before you sign, most wallet apps simulate the transaction and show a preview of how your balances would change. Read it every time. If the preview shows tokens leaving that you did not expect, mentions an owner change or permissions, or the wallet warns that it could not simulate the transaction, reject it. Phantom, for example, shows "This dApp could be malicious" when it cannot simulate a transaction accurately. The preview is a strong defense but not a perfect one, since a malicious site can try to change what happens between the preview and the real execution, so the safest habit is still to sign only on sites you reached on your own.

05Common scams

Almost every theft follows one of a few patterns. None of them breaks Solana itself; they all work by getting you to reveal your seed phrase or sign something.

Fake support and urgent messages

Someone writes to you as a wallet's support team, a moderator or a security service. The story varies (your wallet is compromised, you must verify it, you must move your funds now), but it always ends with a request for your seed phrase or a link to a site that asks for it. Real support teams never contact you first, and they never need your seed phrase.

Fake sites and drainers

A drainer is a site built to make you sign a transaction that empties your wallet, like the one in fig. 2. People reach these sites through ads in search results, look-alike domain names, hacked social media accounts of real projects or links in Discord and Telegram. The bait is usually something free: an airdrop, a mint, a reward to claim.

Tokens you did not ask for

On Solana anyone can send you a token, so wallets fill up with unknown tokens and NFTs whose names include a link or a message like "visit this site to claim your reward". The token itself is harmless; the site it points to is not. Ignore them, or hide them in your wallet, and never visit the link.

Address poisoning

Attackers generate addresses that start and end with the same characters as an address you use, then send you a tiny or empty transfer from it so that it appears in your history. Because wallets shorten addresses, the fake one looks identical, and the next time you copy "your friend's address" from the history, you may copy the attacker's.

The scale is large: an analysis by Bitquery counted 4.5 million Solana wallets that received transfers from look-alike addresses over 11 months. The defense is simple: never copy an address from your transaction history. Use your wallet's address book, or get the address again from its owner, and compare the whole address, not only the beginning and the end.

06Hands-on: a seed phrase in the terminal

If you installed the Solana CLI, you can see the relationship between a seed phrase and an address yourself. Create a throwaway keypair:

bash
solana-keygen new --no-bip39-passphrase --outfile ~/demo.json

The output shows the address (pubkey) and the 12-word seed phrase. Now rebuild the keypair from the words alone, typing them when the CLI asks for the seed phrase and pressing Enter at the passphrase question:

bash
solana-keygen recover --outfile ~/recovered.json

Before writing anything, the CLI shows the address it recovered, and it is the same one. The same words, used with the same method, always produce the same address on any computer, which is exactly how a wallet is restored on a new phone. It is also why anyone who reads your words can do the same.

[i] wallet apps may show a different address

Wallet apps and the CLI can derive accounts from a seed phrase in slightly different ways. If you import these words into a wallet app, it may show a different address. Each tool is consistent with itself.

[!] use this keypair for practice only

A seed phrase that has been shown on a screen, recorded or copied anywhere should never hold real funds. When you finish, delete both files with rm ~/demo.json ~/recovered.json.

07Summary

Your wallet holds keys, and the keys come from your seed phrase, so protecting that phrase is the same as protecting your funds. Most losses do not come from broken cryptography but from people signing something they did not understand or typing their phrase where they should not. Six habits prevent nearly all of them:

  1. Write your seed phrase on paper or metal, keep it offline, and never type it anywhere except your wallet's restore screen.
  2. Never share your seed phrase or private key, whoever asks.
  3. Reach sites by typing the address or using your own bookmarks, not through ads, messages or unknown links.
  4. Read the wallet preview before every signature, and reject anything you did not expect.
  5. Never copy addresses from your transaction history; compare the whole address before sending.
  6. Keep larger amounts on a hardware wallet, and use a separate wallet with small amounts for trying new apps.
